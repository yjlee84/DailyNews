Nvidia’s board said on Sunday that it has opened an independent investigation into Chief Executive Jensen Huang’s relationship with Alice Huang, an executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore-based Nvidia customer. The two are not related. US intelligence agencies reportedly believe she was recruited by China’s Ministry of State Security to gather information on him. A committee of independent directors, assisted by outside lawyers, will lead the inquiry. The board said it has “no reason to believe that either national security or NVIDIA secrets were compromised,” but that its inquiry “will examine all available information.” In an email to staff the same day, Huang admitted an affair, writing “I was unfaithful to my wife,” and said he supports the investigation and will cooperate.

The allegations come from reporting by The New York Times, which said a memo circulated among US policymakers earlier this year identified Alice Huang as a recruit of Chinese intelligence. US officials have scrutinized Megaspeed over suspicions that it helped Chinese companies get around US export limits on advanced AI chips. Nvidia had earlier described Huang’s contacts with her as normal dealings with a customer, and China’s foreign ministry called the Times report “groundless speculation.” Huang remains chief executive; a company spokesperson said, “Today’s news will not slow NVIDIA.” The Republican chairs of three House committees have asked Huang to attend a closed-door briefing on November 18, and Senator Chris Coons, a Democrat, said “Congress will investigate and get answers.”

## Why it matters

This is today’s highest-impact story because Nvidia dominates the global market for the chips that power artificial intelligence, and its chief executive sits at the center of the US–China contest over who gets them. Any finding that Chinese intelligence reached him would bear on US export policy and on an industry whose stocks fell sharply last week. What the intelligence assessment actually says has not been made public, and Nvidia insists nothing was compromised. What to watch: Nvidia’s quarterly results on November 17, the congressional briefing a day later, and what the board’s inquiry finds.
