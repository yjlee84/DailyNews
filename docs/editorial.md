# Editorial guide

Rules for everything published on euleridentity.com.

## Voice

- No first-person pronouns (I, we, my, our). Opinions are phrased as judgments: "The book is right that…", not "I think…".
- Plain, specific language. Figures over adjectives.
- Headlines of briefs and articles have no closing period. Brand and section names keep it: "euleridentity.", "Daily News.", "Productivity Lab.".

## Daily News

**One story per day: the highest-impact story, not the easiest to source.** Score candidates on:

| Test | Question |
|---|---|
| Reach | How many people does it affect, directly or through prices, jobs, safety or rights? |
| Severity | How much does it change their lives? |
| Duration | Will it still matter in a month or a year? |
| Change | Did something actually move today: a decision, escalation, deal or data release? |

Search world news and markets broadly before choosing. Ease of sourcing decides how a brief is written, never which story is picked. When nothing definite happened on the top story, write a "where things stand" brief that separates what is confirmed from what is reported, unless that storyline is already at its weekly limit (see below).

**Variety:** readers should not get the same story every day, whatever the subject is. The rules below apply to any topic, today's or a future one. Storylines are named by the agent when it writes a brief and stored in the brief (see "Labels are stored").

- **Storyline:** one running subject together with everything that follows from it. A brief belongs to the same storyline as an earlier one if a reader would call it "more of the same story", including price moves, reactions, analysis and spin-offs that happen because of it. Examples: a war, its blockade and the oil price it moves are one storyline. A central bank's rate path and the market reaction to it are one storyline. A rocket programme's test flights and its launch delays are one storyline.
- **Topic area:** the broad field a storyline sits in. Use exactly one of: Conflict and diplomacy, Economy and markets, Health, Science and space, Technology, Climate and disasters, Politics and law, Society and culture, Crime and security, Other. Use Other only when none of the first nine fits, so that a new kind of subject is never forced into the wrong area. Name the area for each candidate.
- **Labels are stored, not re-derived:** every brief carries `storyline:` and `topic:` in its front matter, set once by the run that writes it. Later runs read the labels from the last seven briefs and never relabel them. When labelling today's story, first look at the storyline names already used in the last seven briefs. If the story is "more of the same", reuse that exact name. Invent a new name (a short noun phrase, for example "Rate hikes and inflation") only for a genuinely new subject.
- **Storyline limit:** at most **two briefs per storyline in any seven days**, counting the briefs from the previous six days, and not on consecutive days.
- **Topic-area limit:** at most **three briefs per topic area in any seven days**, not counting Other (the storyline limit still applies to Other). This one is a strong preference: break it only when no candidate from another area is close in impact, and say so in the note.
- **Major new event:** the storyline limit lifts only for a decision, escalation, deal, attack, discovery or data release that changes the situation, not another statement, reaction or market move that follows from it. A storyline at its limit can still win with such an event, and the note must say what the event was.

**Slow stories:** the Change test does not rule out a serious story the site has not covered in the past seven days. A story that scores very high on reach and severity (for example a growing outbreak or a famine) may qualify with new figures or a new development from the last few days, even if nothing dramatic happened in the last 24 hours. Change stays required for a storyline already covered this week.

**Format** (about two minutes to read):

1. Headline, no closing period
2. Date row
3. Photo, 4:3, with a short credit
4. Two paragraphs of facts, with figures
5. "Why it matters": one paragraph, starting "This is today’s highest-impact story because…", ending with what is still uncertain or what to watch
6. "Read more": two or three source links, each labeled with the name of the outlet whose page it opens. If that page carries a wire story, the label is still the outlet ("PBS NewsHour", not "AP" or "AP via PBS NewsHour"). One exception: a Reuters story is labeled "Reuters" wherever it is hosted, as long as the page itself credits Reuters. Credit the agency in the text when a claim depends on it ("according to Reuters").

No bold lead or summary paragraph, no inline source tags, no ticker chips.

**Sourcing:** check every fact against at least two independent sources. Attribute anything that comes from unnamed officials. Leave out figures (such as prices) that cannot be confirmed twice.

## Productivity Lab

- Articles and reviews stay relevant for years; prefer depth over speed.
- Book reviews: summary, evaluation, recommendation. Describe how the book progresses rather than listing "Part 1, Part 2". Check quotes against the actual text.
- Explain jargon in footnotes, not by rewriting the sentence.
- Don't promote Jaild inside articles. It has its own card on the Lab page.

## Photos

- Only freely licensed images, usually from Wikimedia Commons: public domain, CC0, CC BY or CC BY-SA.
- Credit format: "Place, year. Photo: Name, License." with the name linked to the source page and the license linked to its deed.
- Public domain (e.g. NASA): "Photo: NASA." with no license text.
- Never use a photo in a way that implies endorsement, and never use agency logos.
- Book covers in reviews are acceptable as commonly used for review purposes.
